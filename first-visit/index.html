Why this demo

I haven't worked in EVV. This is how I'd learn a new domain on day one: follow one user through one visit, list what breaks, turn each failure into a requirement, trace it to its source (law, state rule, clinical safety or plain design), and model the visit as a state machine with guarded transitions. I researched the regulation with AI tools, and every regulatory statement in the video links to a primary source below.

User story

As a caregiver on a first visit, I see only what this visit needs, so I arrive prepared and the record stays true.

Personas

Maria, a new caregiver (agency employee). Walter K., 82, Medicaid personal care, uses a walker, latex allergy, hard of hearing in the right ear. His wife Ruth K. lives in the same house and is also a client. The office coordinator. Walter's daughter Linda as the family contact.

Decision: the app suggests, the caregiver confirms each task. Residual risk: tapping through suggestions without reading them. Control: no "confirm all" button; "not done" needs a reason. CMS documentation guidance warns that content populated by default may document services that were never delivered and advises against notes that need no action from their author (used here by analogy: the guidance targets clinical EHR notes).

The regulation behind it

  • Six EVV elements. Federal law defines electronic visit verification for Medicaid personal care and home health visits as verifying "the type of service performed; the individual receiving the service; the date of the service; the location of service delivery; the individual providing the service; and the time the service begins and ends" (42 U.S.C. 1396b(l)(5)(A), added by section 12006 of the 21st Century Cures Act). The statute names no technology; CMS says GPS is not required.
  • Aggregators and edits. When agencies use different EVV systems, CMS describes a state data aggregator that combines their data; CMS says states should set rules for how visit records are edited. Reason codes for manual fixes come from state rules (for example the Texas EVV Policy Handbook, Ohio and Arizona guidance).
  • Claim matching. In Ohio, Medicaid claims that require EVV must match a visit in the EVV system, or they are denied (phased in March 2025 – March 2026). How EVV data is checked against claims is each state's choice.
  • Minimum necessary. HIPAA asks covered entities to make reasonable efforts to limit protected health information to the minimum necessary, and to limit workforce access by role (45 CFR 164.502(b), 164.514(d)). The trimmed visit card is a design choice in line with that principle, not something HIPAA prescribes field by field.
